He drew a line on a weather map pointing straight at New York, and his boss erased it. You see, it's September 1938. A hurricane is sitting off the coast of Florida, and every forecaster in the building agrees it will curve out to sea. They always do. Nobody in New England has seen a hurricane in a hundred and twenty years. He's the youngest man in the room, filling in for the day. And his line doesn't curve. It goes north. Fast. "Son, hurricanes don't go to Boston." The official forecast goes out: rain, gusty winds. The newspapers print it. Fishermen head out. Families pack the beach for the last swim of summer. The next morning, Long Island wakes up to a grey, ordinary drizzle, and his boss walks past his desk whistling. Meanwhile, out at sea, the storm has slipped into a river of wind that's shoving it north at sixty miles an hour. Faster than any hurricane anyone had ever measured. By the time the first wave came over the dunes, it was too late to send a telegram. People on the beach thought the wall of water was a fog bank. It was taller than their houses. By dinner, nearly seven hundred people were dead, and the forecast on the kitchen table still said rain.

## Story (true events, told lightly)
1911, the Louvre: the Mona Lisa is just one small painting among hundreds in the crowded Salon Carré; people walk past. The director
boasts that stealing it is as impossible as stealing the towers of Notre-Dame. Vincenzo Peruggia, a small Italian glazier who had
installed protective glass on Louvre paintings (including this one), is mocked by French co-workers as "ไอ้มักกะโรนี". Sunday night he
hides in a storage closet; Monday the museum is closed and only workers in white smocks walk around. Monday morning he puts on the same
white smock, lifts the painting off the wall, unframes it in a service staircase, hides the panel under his smock. The exit door is locked;
he sweats until a passing plumber opens it for him and even greets him. All day nobody notices; guards think the photographers took it.
Next day a painter comes to paint there and asks where it is; Notre-Dame's towers still stand, but the Mona Lisa is gone. Louvre closed a
week, police search everything, even question Picasso — nobody suspects the macaroni guy. When the Louvre reopens, crowds queue to stare at
the empty wall with four iron hooks. Her face is in newspapers worldwide, songs, postcards, adverts. The real painting lies in a wooden
trunk under his bed in a small rented room, two years. 1913 he offers it to an art dealer in Florence, who comes to look and brings police.
Reveal: the painting nobody looked at became the one the whole world wanted to see, because it vanished. Ending: today tens of thousands a
day queue to see her, still smiling behind glass, just like the day the macaroni guy first fitted her glass.
